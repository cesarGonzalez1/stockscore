**Original Altman Z-score (1968, publicly held manufacturers)**

Z = 1.2X1 + 1.4X2 + 3.3X3 + 0.6X4 + 1.0X5

- X1 = working capital / total assets
- X2 = retained earnings / total assets
- X3 = EBIT / total assets
- X4 = market value of equity / book value of total liabilities
- X5 = sales / total assets

Zones: Z > 2.99 is "safe," 1.81 < Z < 2.99 is "grey," and Z < 1.81 is "distress."

**Private (Z') and non-manufacturer/emerging-market (Z'') variants**

- **Z' (private companies):** The page names this variant but does not give its coefficients or zone thresholds. Its X4 uses book value of equity / total liabilities.
- **Z'' (non-manufacturers):** Z = 6.56X1 + 3.26X2 + 6.72X3 + 1.05X4. The page gives no zone thresholds for this model.
- **Emerging-markets model:** Z = 3.25 + 6.56X1 + 3.26X2 + 6.72X3 + 1.05X4. Zones: Z > 2.6 is "safe," 1.1 < Z < 2.6 is "grey," and Z < 1.1 is "distress."

**Reported accuracy**

- Initial test (Altman, 1968): 72% accurate two years before bankruptcy, with a Type II error of 6%.
- Later tests (Altman, 2000), covering periods up to 1999: roughly 80–90% accurate one year before bankruptcy, with a Type II error of roughly 15–20%.

**Limitations**

- The page says the accuracy figures overstate predictive ability. Critics call the original work "largely descriptive" and note it shows that failed and non-failed firms have different ratios, not that the ratios predict failure.
- The original sample was small: 66 manufacturers, half of which filed for bankruptcy.
- The page notes the page's Type II error definition conflicts with its earlier "false negatives" wording.
- The model is not recommended for financial companies because of their opaque balance sheets and off-balance-sheet items.
- Modern academic models rely more heavily on market-based data than on the accounting ratios the Z-score uses.

**Original Jegadeesh and Titman findings:** The page calls their work the oldest and most well-known study on the topic. Its referenced abstract says stocks that performed best (worst) over three to 12 months tended to keep performing well (poorly) over the following three to 12 months. It also reports that these strategies were consistently profitable in the U.S. and most developed markets, and that high earnings momentum outperformed low earnings momentum.

**Momentum crashes and caveats:**
- A pure long-short momentum portfolio had a disastrous 2009, with more than 80% drawdown, according to Kenneth French data cited on the page.
- Barroso and Santa-Clara note that momentum offered the highest Sharpe ratio among the market, value, and size factors, but also "the worst crashes." Their risk-managed version "nearly doubles the Sharpe ratio."
- Crashes tend to occur as markets rebound after large declines, linked to momentum's time-varying negative beta after bear markets.
- Long-only momentum is more profitable than the short side (Griffin, Ji, and Martin), so it is more accessible to institutions.
- Momentum offers little hedging value in bear markets, since the pure long-only version cannot serve as a hedge.
- Some references report weaker recent results: Bhattacharya et al. find momentum profits insignificant since the late 1990s, and Abhyankar et al. find U.S. returns about 50% lower over 1996–2016.
- Transaction costs are excluded from the headline results, and the strategy is rated "Complex" with "Strong" confidence in the anomaly's validity.
